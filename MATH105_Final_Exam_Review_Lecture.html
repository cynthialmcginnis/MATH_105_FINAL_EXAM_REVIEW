1Simple interest: find the rate

Key idea

Simple interest grows by the same dollar amount every year. Solve I = Prt for r, then convert the decimal to a percent.

r = I / (P · t)

Worked example

Marcus borrowed $4,000 from the credit union for 3 years at simple interest. He paid $600 in interest. What was the annual interest rate?

  1. r = 600 / (4000 · 3)
  2. r = 600 / 12,000 = 0.05
  3. Move the decimal two places: 5%
Answer: 5%

2Percent increase and decrease

Key idea

Percent change always divides by the OLD value. Subtract first, divide second, then convert to a percent.

% change = (new − old) / old

Worked example

  1. Change = 4.32 − 4.15 = 0.17
  2. 0.17 / 4.15 = 0.04096…
  3. ≈ 4.1% increase
Answer: 4.1%

3Find the original after a percent increase

Key idea

The current number already includes the increase. Divide by (1 + r). Do not subtract the percent from the new number.

original = new / (1 + r)

Worked example

Monthly transactions at the base exchange grew 25% this year. There are now 15,000 per month. How many were there a year ago?

  1. original = 15,000 / 1.25
  2. original = 12,000
  3. Check: 12,000 × 1.25 = 15,000 ✓
Answer: 12,000

whole = part / percent

Never compare the coupons directly. Find the final price under each coupon, then subtract the two prices.

sale price = price × (1 − r)

  3. B is lower by 49.60 − 47.00 = $2.60

6Markups and discounts

Key idea

A markup multiplies by (1 + r). A discount multiplies by (1 − r). Apply them one at a time, in order.

price = wholesale × (1 + markup) × (1 − discount)

Worked example

A grill has a wholesale price of $380. Store 1 marks it up 25%. Store 2 marks it up 50%, then gives members 15% off. Where do you pay more?

  1. Store 1: 380 × 1.25 = $475.00
  2. Store 2: 380 × 1.50 = $570.00
  3. Then 570 × 0.85 = $484.50
  4. You pay more at Store 2.
Answer: Store 2 ($484.50)

8Compound interest

Key idea

n is the number of compounding periods per year: 12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly, 365 for daily. Interest earned is the final amount minus what you put in.

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Worked example

Priya invests $3,000 at 2.4% compounded monthly for 4 years. Find the amount and the interest earned.

  1. A = 3000(1 + 0.024/12)^(12·4)
  2. A = 3000(1.002)^48 = $3,301.96
  3. Interest = 3,301.96 − 3,000 = $301.96
Answer: $3,301.96; $301.96

9Future value of an annuity

Key idea

Use this when the same payment goes in at the end of every period and you want the total at the end.

FV = PMT · [(1 + r/n)^(nt) − 1] / (r/n)

Worked example

Marcus deposits $75 at the end of each month into an annuity paying 4.2% compounded monthly. What is it worth in 10 years?

  1. r/n = 0.042/12 = 0.0035; nt = 120
  2. FV = 75[(1.0035)^120 − 1] / 0.0035
  3. FV = $11,160.98
Answer: $11,160.98

10Annuity payment for a savings goal

Key idea

Same annuity formula, solved for the payment. You know the goal (FV) and need the deposit that reaches it.

PMT = FV · (r/n) / [(1 + r/n)^(nt) − 1]

Worked example

Kenji wants $8,000 for a PCS move in 4 years. His ordinary annuity earns 4.8% compounded annually. What yearly payment does he need?

  1. r/n = 0.048; nt = 4
  2. PMT = 8000(0.048) / [(1.048)^4 − 1]
  3. PMT = $1,861.62 per year
Answer: $1,861.62

11Amortized loans

Key idea

Take the down payment off first. The loan amount is what is left. Then use the amortization formula for the monthly payment.

PMT = P(r/n) / [1 − (1 + r/n)^(−nt)]

Worked example

Marcus buys a kei car for $9,500 with 10% down. The rest is a 3-year loan at 5.4% with monthly payments. Find the down payment, loan, and payment.

  1. Down: 9,500 × 0.10 = $950
  2. Loan: 9,500 − 950 = $8,550
  3. PMT = 8550(0.0045) / [1 − (1.0045)^−36]
  4. PMT = $257.79 per month
Answer: $950; $8,550; $257.79

Section C. Exponential Functions

12Reading an exponential model

Key idea

In y = a · b^t, a is the starting value (t = 0) and b is the growth or decay factor. A factor below 1 means the value shrinks.

v(t) = a · b^t v(0) = a

Worked example

The value of a motorcycle t years old is v(t) = 15,000(0.82)^t. Find the initial value and the value after 6 years, to the nearest dollar.

  1. Initial: v(0) = 15,000(0.82)^0 = $15,000
  2. v(6) = 15,000(0.82)^6
  3. v(6) = 4,560.10… ≈ $4,560
Answer: $15,000; $4,560

13Mean, median, and mode

Key idea

Mean: add and divide. Median: sort first, then take the middle. Mode: the value that repeats most. A data set can have zero, one, or two modes.

mean = Σx / n

Worked example

Data: 12, 45, 30, 45, 18, 30, 52. Find the mean, median, and mode(s).

  1. Mean: 232 / 7 = 33.14… ≈ 33.1
  2. Sorted: 12, 18, 30, 30, 45, 45, 52
  3. Median: 30 (4th value)
  4. Modes: two modes, 30 and 45
Answer: 33.1; 30; modes 30 and 45

14Score needed for a target mean

Key idea

Multiply the target mean by the total number of tests. That is the total you need. Subtract what you already have.

needed = target × n − (sum so far)

Worked example

Yuki scored 72, 85, and 78 on three tests. What does she need on the fourth test to average 80?

  1. Total needed: 80 × 4 = 320
  2. Sum so far: 72 + 85 + 78 = 235
  3. Needed: 320 − 235 = 85
Answer: 85

15Choosing mean, median, or mode

Key idea

Outlier present: median. Data clustered with no outlier: mean. Categories, or 'most common': mode.

outlier → median | even spread → mean | categories → mode

Worked example

Pick the best measure. (a) Home prices with one mansion. (b) The most common shoe size sold. (c) Quiz scores 70–88 spread evenly, no outliers.

  1. (a) The mansion drags the mean up → median
  2. (b) 'Most common' → mode
  3. (c) No outlier, numbers close together → mean
Answer: median; mode; mean

16Comparing groups with percents

Key idea

'All but 6' means subtract 6 from the group size. Compare percents, not raw counts, because the groups are different sizes.

percent = (count who did) / (group size)

Worked example

40 Marines took a fitness test; all but 6 passed. 60 sailors took it; all but 12 passed. Which group did worse?

  1. Marines: 34 / 40 = 85%
  2. Sailors: 48 / 60 = 80%
  3. Sailors did worse: 80% vs. 85%
Answer: Sailors (80% vs. 85%)

20Representative samples

Key idea

The best sample is chosen at random from the WHOLE population. Samples limited to one location or subgroup are biased.

estimate = (sample proportion) × population

Worked example

Estimate students who commute 30+ minutes. Options: 30 students in the library at 7 AM; 30 in one online section; 30 randomly chosen from all 2,400 students (12 commute 30+).

  1. Best: random from ALL students
  2. 12 / 30 × 2,400
  3. = 960 students
Answer: 960

22Ranking probabilities

Key idea

Compute each probability as a fraction. A color that is not in the box has probability 0. 'Either color' from a box of only those colors has probability 1.

P(event) = favorable / total

Worked example

Box A: 6 red, 14 green. Box B: 5 red, 5 green. Rank: (1) red from B, (2) yellow from A, (3) red or green from A, (4) red from A.

  1. (1) 5/10 = 0.5 (2) 0/20 = 0
  2. (3) 20/20 = 1 (4) 6/20 = 0.3
  3. Least → most likely: 2, 4, 1, 3
Answer: 2, 4, 1, 3

23Odds in favor and against

Key idea

Odds compare outcomes to outcomes, not outcomes to the total. In favor = win : lose. Against = lose : win. Reduce like a fraction.

in favor = w : l against = l : w

Worked example

A raffle has 10 prizes: 3 gift cards, 5 hats, 2 speakers. Find the odds in favor of and against winning a gift card.

  1. Gift cards: 3. Not gift cards: 7
  2. In favor: 3 : 7
  3. Against: 7 : 3
Answer: 3:7; 7:3

24Combinations vs. permutations

Key idea

Ask one question: does order matter? A group or a pizza: no, use combinations. A ranking or named offices: yes, use permutations.

nCr = n! / [r!(n − r)!] nPr = n! / (n − r)!

Worked example

(a) How many 4-topping pizzas from 9 toppings? (b) 20 runners: how many ways to rank the top 3?

  1. (a) Order doesn't matter: 9C4 = 126
  2. (b) Order matters: 20P3 = 20 · 19 · 18
  3. = 6,840
Answer: 126; 6,840
